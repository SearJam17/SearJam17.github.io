What Makes a Quality Shot?

Using 1,004 recorded shot attempts from a collegiate basketball team, I built a logistic-regression model that turns shot context into expected points per shot. The clearest finding: how closely a shot is defended matters far more than its zone or the shot clock.

The problem

A professor who leads a team of student analysts asked me to lead this analysis and report the findings. The brief: define what a quality shot is, regardless of who takes it, using shot type, location, zone, contest level and shot clock. A coaching staff can use the answer to judge possessions by the look they create, not just by whether the ball went in.

I defined quality as expected points per shot (PPS): the chance a shot goes in, multiplied by what it's worth. The "weights" come from a model, not from guesswork.

Data and checks

  • Dataset: 1,004 shot attempts and 10 columns, with no missing values. Recorded points matched make × shot value on every shot.
  • A hidden data error: Excel had converted two shot-clock labels into dates ("10-6" became October 6 and "5-0" became May 2000). I mapped them back once at load time and checked the mapping with date arithmetic.
  • Baselines: 1.021 points per shot across all included players' attempts, and 1.08 for the like-for-like population the model covers (after removing excluded players, blocked shots and mid-range twos). Model results are compared with 1.08.
  • Model scope: non-blocked attempts from the paint and from three by the included players (about 900 shots). Blocked shots were excluded because a block describes the outcome, not the situation before the shot. Mid-range twos were excluded because only 10 attempts remained.
  • Privacy: player names were left out for privacy. This page reports team-level results, and the full report shows players only as anonymous labels (Player 01, Player 02, ...).

Approach

  1. Descriptive pass: PPS and effective field-goal percentage by zone, shot clock and contest level.
  2. Model: a logistic regression of make/miss on shot type, zone, contest level and shot-clock window. Rare shot types (under 20 attempts) were grouped. Detailed location was left out because each location sits inside exactly one zone, which made the two redundant.
  3. Whole-variable tests: likelihood-ratio tests dropping one characteristic at a time, since no single category row can show whether a whole variable matters.
  4. Validation: calibration by predicted-probability decile, then 10-fold cross-validation against a contest-only model and a null model.
  5. Expected PPS: predicted make probability × point value for every observed shot context, with 95% intervals and low-sample flags.

Findings

1. Defensive pressure is the clearest signal

After adjusting for shot type, zone and shot clock, a highly contested shot has about one-third the odds of going in compared with a lightly contested one (odds ratio 0.33, 95% interval 0.22–0.48). Wide-open and lightly contested shots couldn't be reliably separated from each other.

2. The paint isn't worse than the three-point line. It's just more contested.

Pooled together, threes returned slightly more than paint attempts. At the same level of pressure, paint shots return as much or more: 1.40 vs 1.37 expected PPS when wide open, and 0.66 vs 0.50 when highly contested. The paint looked weaker only because 39% of paint attempts were highly contested, compared with 16% of above-the-break threes.

3. The shot clock isn't the problem in itself

Raw scoring falls late in the possession, but that difference largely disappears once contest level and shot type are included. Late shots look worse mainly because they tend to be tougher looks.

4. The model holds up on shots it hasn't seen

  • 0.653cross-validated AUC (contest-only model: 0.583)
  • 0.227Brier score vs 0.249 for guessing the average make rate
  • 4.2 ptsaverage in-sample calibration gap; no decile outside chance range

The gain is real but modest, which is normal for shot data: even good looks miss often.

Recommendations

  • Judge the look, not the result. A wide-open or lightly contested shot in the paint or from three is a good possession even when it misses.
  • Cut contested paint attempts. They were the most common low-value shot (191 attempts at about 0.66 expected PPS). An open or lightly contested three is worth roughly twice as much.
  • Use ball movement to beat the contest, not the clock. Drive-and-kick, ball reversal and screening matter because they create cleaner looks before the possession breaks down.
  • Treat the context rankings as hypotheses to test in film and practice. The intervals are wide.

StatusThese are recommendations from the analysis, not changes that have been implemented.

Limitations

  • Observational data: the results are adjusted associations, not causal effects.
  • "Regardless of shooter" doesn't remove shooter skill. Players take different shot mixes, so this is a pooled description of one team's sample.
  • Cross-validation used random folds, so it estimates performance on new shots from the same players, not on new players or a new season. The file has no game ID, which rules out a game-by-game test.
  • Next data I'd request: a game ID per shot, the original scorer records for the shot-clock labels, pre-shot contest level for blocked attempts, and more mid-range attempts.
